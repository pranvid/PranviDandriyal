01

The problem

Five products, five eras of front-end decisions, one recruiter stuck switching between them.

→

→
Reporting
→
Insights

A typical hiring workflow crossed four of the five products.

TC for projects and candidates, iAssess for assessment delivery, Reporting and Insights for analytics, Smart Interview for recorded interviews. A single hiring task could route a recruiter through two or three entirely different interfaces.

The move to a new Talent Intelligence (TI) platform was the chance to fix more than one experience: it could set how every future SHL product looks, feels and behaves.

  • Disconnected UI languages. The same action looked and worked differently in each product.
  • Inconsistent accessibility. Focus, contrast and keyboard behaviour varied product to product.
  • Repeated work. Every team rebuilt the same patterns from scratch.
  • Lost trust. The interface changed so much between products that some users read it as a security concern.
  • No way to scale. Visual styles couldn't be maintained across product lines, let alone themed per client.

04

The strategy

Three ways forward. Only one fixed the cause instead of the symptoms.

Simply updating the existing products wouldn't solve the deeper issues of consistency, performance and scalability. So the call was made to build the new Talent Intelligence platform from scratch, with a unified design system at its core. The open question was how to build the system itself without stalling the platform behind it.

Option ARejected

Restyle the legacy products

Paint a shared look over each product's existing front end.

Why not
Five codebases would keep drifting apart. It fixes how things look, not how they behave, and does nothing for accessibility or theming.
Option BRejected

Build every component from zero

Design and engineer a bespoke library, pattern by pattern.

Why not
Months of re-solving problems mature open-source systems had already solved, including the accessibility behaviour, before the platform could ship a screen.
Option CChosen

Adapt proven foundations, own the layer on top

Carbon for modular foundations, Material UI for flexible components, customised through SHL's own tokens.

Trade-off we accepted
Living within two libraries' conventions, and tracking their updates, in exchange for speed and battle-tested behaviour. Our tokens sit on top, so the brand never depends on either.

Principles written for HR software, not borrowed

“Be accessible” and “be consistent” are true of every system and settle no arguments. We wrote principles specific to what SHL sells and who uses it, so they could actually decide between two designs.

Principle 01

Accessible by necessity, not by audit

Candidates complete assessments under high-stakes, sometimes legally scrutinised conditions. WCAG 2.1 AA had to live in the tokens and components from day one, not in a later compliance pass.

Principle 02

Credible for high-stakes decisions

Recruiters make hiring calls on this surface. Every pattern had to read as authoritative and unambiguous: enterprise-grade, not consumer-cute.

Principle 03

White-label ready

Enterprise clients routinely ask for their own branding. Every token and component had to be re-themeable per client, colours, logos and type, without anyone touching the system underneath.

Principle 04

Scalable across a growing platform

Modular and reusable, built for mobile, tablet and desktop, and ready to expand as TI absorbs more of SHL's product line.

Tokens: where the principles become code

Principle 03 decided the architecture. If a client's brand had to change without anyone touching a screen, then no screen could reference a raw colour. So tokens run in layers, and each layer has one job.

06

Anatomy of a component: the Button

The most-used component is where a system earns trust, or loses it.

The Button appears on nearly every screen in the platform, so every ambiguity in it gets multiplied. Its spec runs to five documents: introduction, props and tokens, usage, accessibility and content. It covers five variants, three sizes, four container types and six states. Everything below is rebuilt in HTML from the Figma component's own values, so you can hover, tab and click it.

Button Focused
  1. 1Container. 8px radius, 16px side padding. 40, 32 or 24px tall for large, medium and small. Hugs its content; full width only on mobile or stacked layouts.
  2. 2Leading icon. Optional, 24px, toggled by leadingIcon. Decorative, so screen readers skip it.
  3. 3Label. A verb that predicts the outcome, 1–3 words: “Save changes”, “Download report”.
  4. 4Trailing icon. Optional, toggled by trailingIcon. Icon-only buttons drop the label and must carry an aria-label.
  5. 5Focus ring. A 2px white gap, then a 2px ring in secondary-300, so it reads on any background.

Every variant, every state

States are specified, not left to engineering to guess. Loading reuses the disabled surface and swaps the label for a spinner at a fixed width, so the layout doesn't jump and the button can't be pressed twice. Danger ships in two emphasis levels, primary and secondary, each with an explicit label like “Delete file”.

Usage guidelines

What the component allows, what it explicitly doesn't, and why, backed by do and don't examples so the rule isn't just a sentence nobody reads.

Points to remember

  • One primary per section. The app header, modals and side panels may carry their own.
  • The primary action sits on the right, at the outermost edge; others line up beside it, or stack above it at full width.
  • Auto width on large screens. Full width only on mobile or where a layout needs a consistent column.
  • Labels are verbs that predict the outcome: “Save changes”, not “Submit”. Destructive ones say what they destroy: “Delete account”.
  • Don't use buttons to navigate. If the action takes the user to a new page, it's a link.

Do and don't, side by side, not buried in prose.

Accessibility, specified rather than assumed

The Button has its own accessibility doc, so engineers don't have to infer keyboard behaviour or what a screen reader should say. The rules, as written:

Keyboard

Tab order
Reachable with Tab in logical DOM order, left to right, top to bottom. Disabled buttons are skipped.
Activate
Enter triggers the action. Space triggers it too, on key up.
Escape
No effect on the button itself. Inside a modal it closes the container and returns focus to the element that opened it.
Focus
A visible ring on every variant, with a white gap so it holds up on any background.

Screen readers

Role
Native <button> wherever possible, so no extra ARIA. Otherwise role="button".
Name
The visible label is the accessible name. Icons beside text are decorative and ignored.
Icon only
Must name the action: aria-label="Download report".
Disabled
Native disabled, announced as “Button, disabled”.
Loading
aria-busy="true", announced as “Loading…”.

Beyond the Button: the hard calls

The Button is one of a family: Icon Button, Link Button and Split Button each have their own spec built on the same tokens. But hover, focus and disabled states were the easy 80%. The harder calls were picking one filter pattern that had to work whether a product exposed 3 facets or 30, and one table style that stayed legible from a 4-row summary card to a 900-row candidate list.

09

Reflection

Measure before you argue

The audit turned “it feels inconsistent” into 21 of 117. That number did more for buy-in than any mock-up.

Cross-functional, not design-only

Tech leadership alignment and a named engineering owner mattered as much as any token.

Adapt, don't reinvent

Carbon and MUI were the single biggest accelerant to quality and delivery speed.

Governance is the hard part

Without clear ownership, a design system decays the moment its authors stop watching it.

S15 · write

What I'd do differently

One or two honest sentences: something that didn't go to plan (adoption, a pattern you had to rework, a decision you'd revisit). Senior reviewers look for this.

Consistency is what builds trust: internally between teams, and externally for whoever uses what we ship. More than a component library, the system became the foundation the rest of SHL's Talent Intelligence platform builds on.
